Website Development and Custom Business Software in the UAE: Costs, Timelines and Choosing a Developer
If you run a business in the UAE, a website is no longer just an online brochure. It is often the first place a customer checks whether you are credible, the place where an enquiry becomes a quote, and increasingly the front door to a booking, payment, or customer portal. Custom software becomes relevant when the work behind that front door—quotes, stock, job cards, approvals, reporting—is being held together by spreadsheets, WhatsApp messages, and memory.

The practical question is not “website or software?” but “what is the smallest useful system that solves the problem your business actually has?” Scope determines cost and time far more than the label on the project. This guide explains the differences, what drives cost in UAE Dirhams, how delivery timelines work, how to brief a developer, and how to assess proposals. It is written to be useful whether you work with me or another developer.

Do You Need a Website, a Web Application, or Custom Software?
A website is primarily a publishing and enquiry tool. It tells people what you do, who you serve, and how to contact you. A well-built business website in the UAE should load quickly on a mobile phone, make your services easy to understand, and give a customer a clear next step—call, WhatsApp, enquiry form, booking, or purchase.

A web application is software that happens to run in a browser. Customers or staff log in, and the system holds data, applies rules, and shows different information to different people. A customer portal where a client can see their project status is a web application. An internal job-card system is also a web application, even if only staff use it.

Custom software is the broader category: software built around a workflow that is specific to your business, rather than a generic product you configure. It may include a web application, a mobile-oriented interface, integrations, reporting, and automation.

A hypothetical example makes the distinction clearer. A tour operator might start with a website that describes itineraries and takes enquiries. If the same operator is managing bookings across a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, and a paper diary, the next step is not “a bigger website”. It is a booking and availability workflow—probably a web application—that records enquiries, checks availability, issues confirmations, and produces a simple forward booking report. If the operator later wants suppliers to log in and confirm rates, that is an extension of the same system, not a separate website.

Off-the-shelf software is often the right answer. If your need is accounting, payroll, basic customer relationship management, or standard email marketing, a subscription product will usually be cheaper and faster than building. Custom development makes sense when the workflow is a genuine source of advantage, when existing tools force you to work in a way that loses money or time, or when integration between several tools is the real bottleneck. The decision is not about being “advanced”. It is about whether the software needs to fit your process closely enough to justify the build.

Write down two customer-facing problems and two internal workflow problems. That list is the beginning of a useful brief, whether you hire a developer or not.

How to Build a Business Website in the UAE
A business website project should start with a business goal, not a colour scheme. “Generate qualified enquiries for our services in Dubai and Abu Dhabi” is a goal. “Look modern” is a preference. Both can matter, but the goal should decide the pages, the calls to action, and what you measure after launch.

Pages and content. Most service businesses need a home page, a clear services or products section, proof of work or credentials, an about page, and a contact page. Tourism and hospitality businesses may need room or itinerary pages, availability information, and booking links. Professional services may need detailed service pages that answer common client questions. The content should be written for the reader, not for a search engine. UAE buyers often want to know where you are, what you actually do, who you have worked with, and how quickly you respond. If you cannot show named clients, describe the type of work and the problems you solve without inventing testimonials. Many UAE businesses also need to decide whether the site should be English-only, Arabic-only, or bilingual, because a proper Arabic version is more than translation—the layout mirrors for right-to-left reading and both versions have to be tested.

Domain and hosting ownership. Register the domain in the business’s name, not the developer’s. Keep the registrar account and hosting account accessible to the business owner or a named responsible person. A .ae domain signals local presence, but a .com can also work. Published UAE registrar pricing puts a .ae domain at roughly AED 120–150 a year, with renewals around AED 130–145, and .com from around AED 49–65 a year; these are third-party examples, not my rates, and they should be checked against current suppliers. Business hosting plans commonly range from around AED 100 to AED 3,000 or more a year depending on the plan, support level, and whether it is shared, VPS, or managed. If a developer insists on owning the domain “for convenience”, treat that as a warning sign.

Design and mobile usability. Internet use in the UAE is effectively universal and overwhelmingly mobile. As of 2025, 100% of the population used the internet, 100% had access to at least one 4G mobile device, and 99% had access to a 5G device; around 95% of users access the internet through a mobile phone. A site that is difficult to use on a mid-range Android phone is a business problem, not a cosmetic one. Test the site on a real phone, on a mobile network, not only on office fibre or Wi-Fi. Page speed, readable text, tappable buttons, and forms that work without unnecessary fields matter more than animation.

Contact, booking, payment, and enquiry requirements. A contact form is not automatically useful. Decide where enquiries should go, who responds, and what information is genuinely needed. If you take payments online, you will need a payment gateway that supports UAE Dirhams and your business profile. Common choices in the UAE include Telr, Network International, PayTabs, and Ziina, with some providers also offering payment links, QR payments, and tap-to-pay alongside conventional gateways. Payment integration is not a one-off development task: there are onboarding checks, transaction fees, settlement rules, refund handling, and reconciliation to consider. Ask who owns the gateway account and who is responsible for chargebacks and customer queries.

Analytics and search foundations. At minimum, set up analytics and verify the site in Google Search Console. Make sure each important service has a page that can be indexed, that page titles and descriptions are sensible, and that the site has a clear internal link structure. These are foundations, not guarantees of ranking.

Testing, launch, maintenance, and handover. Before launch, test forms, links, mobile layouts, page speed, and any payment or booking flow. After launch, someone must be responsible for updates, backups, security patches, and content changes. A website is software; an unmaintained site becomes slow, insecure, and dated. Handover should include account credentials, documentation of what was built, and a clear support arrangement.

Website builders, content management systems, and custom development. A website builder can be appropriate for a very small business that needs a clean presence quickly and is willing to work within a template. A content management system such as WordPress can be a good middle path for a business that wants to edit content and extend the site over time, provided it is kept updated and not overloaded with plugins. Custom development is appropriate when the website is tightly coupled to a workflow, integration, or customer portal that generic tools cannot handle well. None of these options is always best. The right choice depends on budget, internal skills, expected growth, and how much the website is part of the operating model.

Website planning checklist: one clear business goal; five to seven essential pages; written content and real photographs; domain and hosting in the business’s name; mobile testing on a real device; enquiry routing and response ownership; analytics and Search Console; a launch test plan; a named person for ongoing updates.

How Much Does a Website Cost in the UAE?
There is no single market price. Published UAE agency guides in 2026 give a useful orientation, but they are examples from specific providers, not an average and not my rates. One guide puts a five-page starter site at AED 999, a small WordPress business site at AED 2,500, and a basic online store from AED 3,999, with an optional care plan at AED 299 a month. A second guide puts a business or SME website at AED 12,000–40,000, an eCommerce store at AED 25,000–120,000+, and a custom web application from around AED 60,000 well into six figures. A third puts a brochure site at AED 3,500–8,000, a business site at AED 8,000–25,000, and eCommerce at AED 15,000–60,000. The difference is not dishonesty; it reflects different scopes, design depths, content responsibilities, and business models. The same finished-looking site can cost very differently depending on whether it is built by a solo specialist, a regional studio, or a large agency with office and account-management overheads.

A responsible quote separates the initial build from ongoing and third-party costs. Initial build usually covers discovery, design, development, basic testing, and launch. It may or may not include copywriting, photography, content migration, or training. Ongoing costs can include domain renewal, hosting, maintenance, backups, security updates, and plugin or platform licences. Payment processing fees are separate and are usually charged per transaction. If the supplier is VAT-registered, quotes may include or exclude VAT; the UAE’s standard VAT rate is 5%, so confirm the basis before comparing totals.

For a service-business website in the UAE, a realistic budget conversation should cover: how many pages, whether content is supplied or created, whether the site needs Arabic as well as English, whether there are bookings or payments, whether the site needs to connect to an existing system, and who will maintain it. A cheap quote that excludes content, mobile testing, analytics, and handover is not necessarily cheap. A high quote that includes strategy, copywriting, integration, training, and support may be better value than a low quote that leaves you with a site you cannot update.

How Much Does Custom Business Software Cost in the UAE?
Custom software pricing is even less standardised than website pricing, but UAE agencies do publish ranges that help with planning. A small, focused tool with a single workflow and one user type typically sits at AED 40,000–150,000, with a timeline of roughly 6–12 weeks. A mid-complexity business platform sits at AED 150,000–500,000, and a genuinely complex, multi-system platform goes well beyond that. Broader guides put custom software projects at AED 60,000–600,000+ depending on scope, and mobile apps at AED 25,000 for simple apps up to AED 400,000+ for moderate or enterprise solutions. These are third-party market examples, not my rates, and they should be treated as planning figures rather than quotes.

The main cost drivers are:

User roles and permissions. A system with one administrator is very different from one with customers, staff, supervisors, and external suppliers seeing different data.

Workflow complexity. Simple create-read-update-delete screens are cheaper than multi-step approvals, exceptions, partial fulfilment, returns, or reconciliation.

Integrations. Connecting to accounting software, banks, payment gateways, SMS or WhatsApp providers, or an existing database adds design, testing, and failure-handling work.

Existing data quality and migration. If current records are in inconsistent spreadsheets or paper files, cleaning and importing them can be a significant part of the project. It is often underestimated.

Reporting. A simple list is not the same as a dashboard with agreed definitions, period comparisons, and export requirements.

Security requirements. Authentication, password reset, audit trails, role separation, and sensitive-data handling all take design and testing time. The UAE’s federal Personal Data Protection Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021) sets conditions for how personal data is collected, processed, stored, and shared, including rules on cross-border transfers. The DIFC and ADGM free zones have their own data protection regimes, which are broadly aligned with GDPR principles but enforced separately. Implementing regulations for the federal law were still pending as of late 2025, and a new national AI and data authority was established in June 2026, so the enforcement picture is still developing. This is not legal advice; a qualified local reviewer should confirm obligations for your specific data and jurisdiction.

Testing. The more roles, integrations, and money flows, the more testing is needed. User acceptance testing with real staff takes calendar time, not just developer time.

Deployment and training. Hosting, environment setup, data migration rehearsal, go-live support, and training all have to be planned.

Support and future changes. A system that is used daily will need changes. Agree what is covered after launch and what is charged separately.

A hypothetical construction workflow shows how to divide scope. A contractor currently tracks projects in a spreadsheet, sends purchase requests by WhatsApp, and keeps delivery notes in a file. An essential first release might include a project register, a purchase-request form with approval by a manager, a delivery-note upload, and a simple project cost report. Later enhancements might include supplier portals, budget-versus-actual alerts, integration with accounting software, and a mobile view for site staff. Ongoing operations include hosting, backups, user support, and periodic changes. Trying to deliver all of that at once increases cost and risk; sequencing it gives the business a working system earlier and a clearer basis for the next decision.

Buying existing software is often preferable. If a standard inventory, accounting, or job-management product already covers 80% of the workflow, the remaining 20% may not justify a custom build. Custom software is most defensible when the workflow is specific, when integration is the main problem, or when the business has outgrown the workarounds. The UAE’s push toward digitisation has increased demand for custom builds, but the businesses that get real value are usually the ones that can name the specific thing existing tools fail at—not the ones reacting to a vague sense that they “should have their own system”.

Describe the current workflow, not the technology stack. A developer who understands the workflow can propose the technology; a brief that starts with “build me a React app” often hides the real problem.

How Long Does It Take to Build Business Software?
Delivery time is a function of scope, decision speed, and dependencies—not just developer effort. A brochure website can be delivered in a few weeks once content and approvals are ready. A custom application is usually a multi-month process, with the first usable release deliberately kept small. Published UAE planning figures suggest a small focused tool at roughly 6–12 weeks, a mid-complexity platform at 3–6 months, and a genuinely complex multi-system platform at 6–12 months or more. These are planning expectations, not promises; the only reliable timeline is one tied to an agreed scope.

The stages are broadly consistent:

Discovery. Understand the business, the users, the current workaround, and the outcome that matters. This is where a good developer asks uncomfortable questions about exceptions and edge cases.

Scope and workflow mapping. Define what the first release must do, what it explicitly will not do, and how success will be judged. A written scope with exclusions is more useful than a feature list.

Design. Map the screens and user journeys. For internal tools, this is often about reducing clicks and making the next action obvious, not visual polish.

Development. Build in small increments and show progress. Waiting until the end to review is a recipe for rework.

Integration. Connect payment, messaging, accounting, or other systems and test failure cases, not just the happy path.

Testing and user acceptance. Real users try real tasks. Issues are logged, prioritised, fixed, and retested.

Deployment and training. Go live with support, documentation, and a plan for the first weeks of use.

The common delays are predictable: slow feedback, missing content, delayed access to third-party systems, poor data quality, changing scope, and approval bottlenecks. A developer can manage the build; they cannot manage your internal decision-making. If a project has a hard launch date, the scope must be fixed early and the client must commit to review windows. If the scope is still evolving, the date should be treated as a target, not a contractual certainty.

Prioritisation exercise: list every feature you think you need, then mark each as “must have for the first release”, “later”, or “not actually necessary”. If more than a third are “must have”, the first release is probably too large.

When Should You Hire an AI Developer?
AI is useful when the task involves language, documents, or unstructured information at a volume that people cannot handle consistently. Common business cases include:

Document search and retrieval. Staff need to find the right clause, policy, or supplier document without reading every file. A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system can search a private document set and return relevant passages with references.

Customer enquiry assistance. A first-line assistant can classify enquiries, suggest replies, or route them to the right person, with human review for anything sensitive or high-value.

Structured data extraction. Converting invoices, forms, or delivery notes into structured records can reduce manual entry, provided the output is validated.

Drafting and quotation support. An assistant can draft a first-pass quotation or proposal from a template and approved pricing rules, leaving the final judgement to a person.

Workflow automation. Some “AI” needs are really rules-based automation. If a task can be handled by a deterministic rule, ordinary software is usually cheaper, more predictable, and easier to audit.

AI outputs need evaluation. A model that is right most of the time can still be wrong in ways that damage a customer relationship or a financial record. Good projects define what a “useful result” looks like, build a test set, measure performance, and keep human review where the consequence of error is high. Permissions matter too: an assistant that can read every document in the business may expose salary or client data to the wrong user. Usage costs—model calls, embedding storage, and monitoring—continue after launch and should be budgeted. In the UAE, AI processing of personal data also falls within the scope of the federal data protection framework, so the same data-minimisation and access-control questions that apply to any customer data apply to AI features.

It also helps to distinguish three different services that are often lumped together as “AI development”. Integrating a model into an existing workflow is not the same as building a private document-retrieval system, which is not the same as training or fine-tuning a model. The first is often the smallest and most useful starting point. The second requires document preparation and retrieval design. The third is rarely the first thing a business needs.

Start with one repetitive task and a clear definition of what a useful result would look like. If you cannot describe the task and the acceptable error rate, the project is not ready for AI.

How Do I Make My Website Number One on Google?
No developer can guarantee first place on Google. Anyone who does is either misunderstanding how search works or saying what they think you want to hear. Search rankings are determined by systems that assess relevance, usefulness, page experience, and many other signals, and they change over time. A new website, a particular performance score, or publishing a single article does not produce a guaranteed ranking.

What you can do is build the foundations that make it easier for search engines to understand and trust your site:

Crawlability and indexing. Make sure search engines can access your pages, that important pages are not blocked, and that you have submitted a sitemap in Google Search Console.

Useful pages that match customer questions. A page for each major service, written in the language customers use, is more useful than one generic “services” page. Answer the questions buyers actually ask: price drivers, timelines, coverage, process, and what happens next.

Clear service information. State what you do, where you operate, who you serve, and how to contact you. Ambiguity does not help a human or a search engine.

Mobile usability and performance. The UAE’s internet use is mobile-heavy, with around 95% of users accessing the internet through a phone. A slow or broken mobile experience undermines both usability and search visibility.

Internal navigation and links. Connect related pages so that a reader can move from a general service page to a detailed explanation, and from an article to the relevant service page.

Credible evidence and relevant mentions. Case studies, project descriptions, professional profiles, and accurate business information help establish credibility. Do not invent client names or results.

Measurement. Track impressions, clicks, enquiries, and qualified leads—not just rankings. A page that ranks for a phrase nobody enquires about is not a business win.

Paid search is separate from organic ranking. Google Ads can put a business in front of buyers quickly, but it is an advertising channel with its own costs and competition. Organic visibility is earned over time through useful content, technical foundations, and credibility. Google is overwhelmingly dominant in the UAE, accounting for around 96% of search engine referrals as of August 2025, so Google Search Console and Google-oriented foundations are the practical starting point for most businesses. Bing and other engines still matter to some audiences, but they should not distract from the basics.

Visibility checklist: verify the site in Google Search Console; submit a sitemap; create a page for each core service; write titles and descriptions that describe the page honestly; test on mobile; add internal links between related pages; track enquiry sources; review search queries every quarter.

How to Choose a Web or Software Developer in the UAE
The right developer is not necessarily the cheapest or the one with the most impressive logo wall. The right developer understands your problem, can explain trade-offs in plain language, and is honest about what they do not know. Use these questions to compare proposals:

Relevant examples. Can they show work that resembles your problem—not just visually, but in workflow and complexity? Ask what was built, what was excluded, and what they would do differently.

Scope and exclusions. Is the scope written down, including what is not included? A proposal that lists only features and no exclusions is incomplete.

Delivery milestones. What will be shown at each stage, and when does the client need to review? “We will deliver at the end” is a risk.

Ownership of code and accounts. Who owns the code, the domain, the hosting account, and the third-party accounts? The business should own or control the critical accounts.

Access credentials. How will access be shared and revoked? Shared passwords in WhatsApp are not an access-control strategy.

Testing and acceptance. What tests will be performed, who signs off, and what happens if something fails?

Maintenance. What is covered after launch, for how long, and at what cost? What is treated as a change request?

Security responsibilities. Who is responsible for updates, backups, access control, and incident response? If the answer is “nobody”, that is a gap.

Documentation and handover. Will you receive documentation, credentials, and a walkthrough? Can another developer take over if needed?

Communication for remote projects. How will progress be reported, how quickly are messages answered, and what time zone overlap is available for calls?

Local and remote delivery both have trade-offs. A local developer can visit the office, meet staff face to face, and understand the informal workflow more easily. A remote developer may offer deeper specialist skills, different cost structures, and flexibility. Neither is automatically better. What matters is clear scope, reliable communication, and a handover that leaves the business in control.

Questions to Answer Before Requesting a Quote
A useful brief does not need to be long, but it should answer these questions:

What does the business do, and who are its customers?

Who will use the solution—customers, staff, suppliers, or a mix?

What currently happens manually, and where does the process break down?

What must the first release include for the business to consider it useful?

Which existing systems must connect—accounting, payments, messaging, inventory, or something else?

What content or data already exists, and in what format?

Is there a budget range? (A range helps a developer propose a realistic scope, even if it is uncomfortable to share.)

Is there a required launch date, and what drives it?

Who will approve decisions and review work?

What ongoing support and maintenance does the business expect?

If you can answer these questions, you will get more useful proposals and waste less time on conversations that were never going to fit.

Frequently Asked Questions
Can I start with a website and add software later?
Yes. Many businesses should. A website that captures enquiries cleanly can later connect to a quotation or booking workflow. The important thing is to avoid building a website that locks you into a structure you will have to throw away. Keep the content and data model simple, and document how enquiries are stored.

Can you improve an existing website?
Often, yes. A redesign or rebuild may be appropriate if the current site is slow, hard to update, or does not reflect the business. Sometimes the better answer is to fix the content, mobile experience, and analytics first. The starting point is a short review of what exists, what works, and what the business needs it to do.

Can a Botswana-based developer work with my business in the UAE?
Yes. I'm based in Botswana and work remotely. The practical considerations are communication, time-zone overlap, access to any local systems, and whether the project benefits from in-person workshops. The UAE is two hours ahead of Botswana, which leaves a workable overlap for scheduled calls and written reviews. These arrangements can be managed with a clear schedule and written decisions.

Who owns the domain, code, and hosting accounts?
The business should own or control the domain and hosting accounts, and the contract should state who owns the custom code. Do not accept an arrangement where the developer owns the domain or holds the only administrator account. Ownership is not a technical detail; it affects your ability to move suppliers.

Will ongoing AI or hosting costs apply?
Usually, yes. Hosting, domain renewal, maintenance, and third-party services are ongoing costs. AI features can add model or API usage costs, document storage, and monitoring. Ask for these to be listed separately from the build fee, with assumptions about usage.

Do I need custom software or an existing subscription tool?
If a standard tool covers most of your workflow at a reasonable cost, use it. Custom software is more defensible when the workflow is specific, when integration is the bottleneck, or when the business has outgrown manual workarounds. A developer should be willing to tell you when off-the-shelf is the better choice.

What should I prepare before asking for a quote?
A short written description of the business, the users, the current process, the first-release must-haves, the systems that need to connect, the content or data you already have, your budget range, and your target date. Even a one-page brief is better than a vague “how much for a website?” message.

Can a developer guarantee first place on Google?
No. No credible developer can guarantee a specific ranking. What can be done is to build a crawlable, useful, mobile-friendly site with clear service pages, measure search visibility, and improve over time. Be sceptical of anyone who promises a ranking as part of a development package.
